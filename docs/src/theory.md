# Theory

## Stochastic Hamiltonian systems

The systems this package integrates are stochastic differential equations in the **Stratonovich**
sense, written with ``\circ``:

```math
dq(t) = v(t, q) \, dt + \sum_{r=1}^{m} B^{\cdot r}(t, q) \circ dW^r(t) .
```

The Stratonovich convention is what makes the geometry available. Its differential obeys the
ordinary chain rule, so a symplectic form, a coordinate change or a conserved quantity behaves as
it does in the deterministic case; in the Itô calculus each picks up a second-order correction and
the structure these integrators preserve would not be there to preserve.

The case of real interest is a **stochastic Hamiltonian system**, with a Hamiltonian ``H`` and one
stochastic Hamiltonian ``h_r`` per noise dimension:

```math
\begin{aligned}
dq &= \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} \, dt
    + \sum_{r} \frac{\partial h_r}{\partial p} \circ dW^r , \\
dp &= -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} \, dt
    - \sum_{r} \frac{\partial h_r}{\partial q} \circ dW^r .
\end{aligned}
```

Its flow is symplectic for every realisation of the noise, pathwise, exactly as in the
deterministic case. A **forced** system adds a deterministic force ``F`` and stochastic forces
``f_r``:

```math
\begin{aligned}
dq &= \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} \, dt
    + \sum_{r} \frac{\partial h_r}{\partial p} \circ dW^r , \\
dp &= \left[ -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} + F \right] dt
    + \sum_{r} \left[ -\frac{\partial h_r}{\partial q} + f_r \right] \circ dW^r ,
\end{aligned}
```

which is no longer symplectic — a damped oscillator must lose energy — but still arises from a
variational principle, the stochastic Lagrange-d'Alembert principle. What a good integrator should
preserve is that *principle*, not the symplectic form.

## Strong and weak convergence

Two quite different things can be meant by "the numerical solution is accurate", and a stochastic
scheme is designed for one or the other.

A scheme converges with **mean-square (strong) order** ``p`` if

```math
\sqrt{ \mathbb{E} \left| q(T) - q_N \right|^2 } \le C \, \Delta t^{\,p} ,
```

that is, it tracks the *individual sample path* generated by the same noise. This is what is
needed to compare against an exact pathwise solution, to study a particular trajectory, or to
refine a path.

A scheme converges with **weak order** ``p`` if

```math
\left| \mathbb{E}\, \varphi(q(T)) - \mathbb{E}\, \varphi(q_N) \right| \le C \, \Delta t^{\,p}
```

for smooth test functions ``\varphi`` — it reproduces *expectations*, and says nothing about any
one path. That is all that is needed for mean energies, ergodic limits or distributions, and it is
a much weaker demand, which is why weak schemes can afford much cheaper randomness.

That cost difference is the whole point. A strong scheme must be fed genuine Gaussian increments;
a weak scheme is derived only against the *moments* of the increments, so it can replace them with
a discrete random variable that matches those moments and costs one uniform draw. See
[Noise Processes](@ref).

!!! warning "The two are not interchangeable"
    Feeding a strong scheme weak increments, or the reverse, produces plausible-looking numbers of
    the wrong accuracy, with no error and no warning. This package therefore derives the choice
    from the method — [`convergence`](@ref) — rather than exposing it as an option. There is no
    way to ask for the wrong one.

### The order 1.0 ceiling

Every method in this package uses only the increments
``\Delta W^r = \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} dW^r``, and possibly the iterated integrals
``\Delta Z^r = \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} \int_{t_n}^{t} dW^r(\xi) \, dt``. A scheme built from
``\Delta W`` alone cannot exceed **mean-square order 1.0**, however many stages it has — the
missing information is in the higher multiple Stratonovich integrals, not in the tableau.

Carrying the ``\Delta Z`` terms is therefore necessary to pass that ceiling, but it is not
sufficient. Of the methods here only [`BurrageCL`](@ref), [`BurrageE1`](@ref) and
[`BurrageG5`](@ref) carry them, and of those three only `BurrageCL` and `BurrageG5` reach order
1.5 — `BurrageE1` uses the same terms and remains of order 1.0.

Order 1.0 further requires the noise to be **commutative**. For a one-dimensional Wiener process
commutativity is automatic; for multi-dimensional non-commutative noise the same schemes drop to
order 0.5.

## Variational integrators

A deterministic variational integrator is derived by discretising the action and applying a
discrete variational principle, rather than by discretising the equations of motion. The resulting
scheme is symplectic and momentum-preserving by construction.

The same construction works in the stochastic setting. Kraus and Tyranowski derive a discrete
stochastic Lagrange-d'Alembert principle and show that a stochastic partitioned Runge-Kutta method

```math
\begin{aligned}
Q_i &= q_n + \Delta t \sum_j a_{ij} \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}(Q_j,P_j)
           + \sum_r \Delta W^r \sum_j b_{ij} \frac{\partial h_r}{\partial p}(Q_j,P_j) , \\
P_i &= p_n - \Delta t \sum_j \bar a_{ij} \frac{\partial H}{\partial q}(Q_j,P_j)
           - \sum_r \Delta W^r \sum_j \bar b_{ij} \frac{\partial h_r}{\partial q}(Q_j,P_j) \\
    &\qquad + \Delta t \sum_j \hat a_{ij} F(Q_j,P_j)
           + \sum_r \Delta W^r \sum_j \hat b_{ij} f_r(Q_j,P_j) ,
\end{aligned}
```

with final update

```math
\begin{aligned}
q_{n+1} &= q_n + \Delta t \sum_i \alpha_i \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}(Q_i,P_i)
              + \sum_r \Delta W^r \sum_i \beta_i \frac{\partial h_r}{\partial p}(Q_i,P_i) , \\
p_{n+1} &= p_n - \Delta t \sum_i \alpha_i \frac{\partial H}{\partial q}(Q_i,P_i)
              - \sum_r \Delta W^r \sum_i \beta_i \frac{\partial h_r}{\partial q}(Q_i,P_i) \\
        &\qquad + \Delta t \sum_i \hat\alpha_i F(Q_i,P_i)
              + \sum_r \Delta W^r \sum_i \hat\beta_i f_r(Q_i,P_i) ,
\end{aligned}
```

is such an integrator precisely when its coefficients satisfy the **Lagrange-d'Alembert
conditions**

```math
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_i \bar a_{ij} + \alpha_j a_{ji} &= \alpha_i \alpha_j , &
\beta_i \bar b_{ij} + \beta_j b_{ji}   &= \beta_i \beta_j , \\
\beta_i \bar a_{ij} + \alpha_j b_{ji}  &= \beta_i \alpha_j , &
\alpha_i \bar b_{ij} + \beta_j a_{ji}  &= \alpha_i \beta_j , \\
\alpha_i \hat a_{ij} + \hat\alpha_j a_{ji} &= \alpha_i \hat\alpha_j , &
\alpha_i \hat b_{ij} + \hat\beta_j a_{ji}  &= \alpha_i \hat\beta_j , \\
\beta_i \hat a_{ij} + \hat\alpha_j b_{ji}  &= \beta_i \hat\alpha_j , &
\beta_i \hat b_{ij} + \hat\beta_j b_{ji}   &= \beta_i \hat\beta_j .
\end{aligned}
```

The first four are the familiar symplecticity conditions of a partitioned Runge-Kutta method,
extended to couple the drift and diffusion tableaus. The last four are new, and they are the
reason the ``p`` equation is **split**: they couple the *forcing* tableaus ``\hat a``, ``\hat b``
to ``a``, ``b``. A method that applied one set of coefficients to the whole right-hand side of the
``p`` equation could not satisfy them in general, which is why forced systems need an `SPSDE` and
a [`SISPRK`](@ref) method rather than a `PSDE` and a [`SIPRK`](@ref).

Mean-square order 1.0 is a separate requirement, and a considerably simpler one:

```math
\sum_i \alpha_i = \sum_i \hat\alpha_i = \sum_i \beta_i = \sum_i \hat\beta_i = 1 ,
```
```math
\sum_{i,j} \beta_i b_{ij} = \sum_{i,j} \beta_i \bar b_{ij} = \sum_{i,j} \beta_i \hat b_{ij}
= \sum_{i,j} \hat\beta_i b_{ij} = \sum_{i,j} \hat\beta_i \bar b_{ij}
= \sum_{i,j} \hat\beta_i \hat b_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2} .
```

Note that the drift matrices ``a``, ``\bar a``, ``\hat a`` do not appear at all — only their
weights, and the six ``\beta``/``\hat\beta`` against ``b``/``\bar b``/``\hat b`` products.

The two sets are independent, and a method can satisfy either without the other:

| method | Lagrange-d'Alembert | order 1.0 |
|:--|:--|:--|
| [`StochasticGLRK`](@ref), [`StochasticDIRK`](@ref) | yes | yes |
| [`StochasticStoermerVerlet`](@ref) | yes | yes |
| [`ModifiedStochasticStoermerVerlet`](@ref) | yes | yes |
| [`StochasticSymplecticEuler`](@ref) | yes | **no**, except for special `h`, `f` |
| [`StochasticLobattoIIIABD2`](@ref) | **no** | yes |

All of this is checked directly on the tableau coefficients by
`scripts/tableau_conditions.jl`, including the two deliberate failures — a run that reported
everything satisfied would be reporting a bug in itself.

## Verification

Two scripts in `scripts/` establish the claims made here, and are the place to look before
trusting a number:

* **`tableau_conditions.jl`** evaluates both sets of conditions above on every tableau in the
  package and asserts the expected outcome, failures included. It is exact and instant, so it
  isolates a transcription error in a tableau from an error in the code that consumes it. Run it
  first.
* **`convergence_order.jl`** measures the mean-square order on the Kubo oscillator, whose exact
  solution is known in closed form, driving each refinement along one common sample path with a
  `GridProcess`.
